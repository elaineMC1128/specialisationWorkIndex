Home Page Assignment 1

Area 1: Operational Guidance

The target audience for this project consists of toddlers with limited language and comprehension skills. They have fewer established mental models and less experience with digital interfaces. Design conventions that appear direct or familiar to adults may therefore not communicate their purpose as clearly to young children. Therefore, the interface is designed to communicate its primary functions through clear visual information rather than relying on written instructions.

However, even with an interface designed for accessibility and ease of use, some users may still require assistance in understanding keyboard controls and key operations. This aspect of the project aims to determine whether operational guidance is truly necessary—and if so, when and to what extent it should be provided—without disrupting the gaming experience or diminishing opportunities for independent exploration.

Prototype 1: No Guidance (Baseline)

This prototype removes instructional guidance entirely and relies only on the interface's own affordance cues, a slow breathing glow on the play button and brief (preliminary) pre-interaction prompts in two modes. Nothing is demonstrated and nothing is explained. This establishes the floor of the comparison: whether a toddler can discover the instrument's basic operation from visual invitation alone, without the interface ever stepping in to show them.

Prototype 2: Proactive Guidance

Proactive guidance was selected to explore whether toddlers should be introduced to unfamiliar operations before they begin independent play. Unlike experienced adult users, young children may not recognise when help is available or know that they need to request it, so this prototype allows the system to initiate support at the beginning of the experience. To avoid the process becoming tedious or overly serious, cartoon characters were therefore introduced as part of an emotional design approach, framing the guidance as a friendly invitation to play rather than a formal set of instructions. Their gestures also communicate actions visually, supporting the project’s low-text interaction approach. Each step focuses on one operation to limit the amount of information presented at once, while the "skip" button preserves the option for the child to step away from the guided experience and explore independently.

Prototype 3: On-Demand Guidance

The on-demand prototype places a persistent question-mark button in the upper-right corner of each page. Selecting it opens a modal window containing the operations relevant to the current mode. Each operation is separated into its own page and demonstrated through static renderings or a short repeated animation. Progress dots support movement through the pages and a close button returns the child to the unchanged activity. This prototype explores user-initiated guidance that remains available without occupying the main play space. It allows an operation to be revisited at the moment it becomes relevant and avoids requiring every child to complete guidance before playing.

Area 2: Spatial Sound Visualisation

This area responds to the project’s purpose of helping children recognise a clear causal relationship between their actions, the sounds produced by the instrument and the resulting visual response. It applies controlled randomisation as the project’s extended technique to explore whether unpredictability can add playfulness and support open-ended exploration without weakening the child’s sense that the visual outcome was caused by their own input.

These three prototypes hold the trigger, the colour, and the temporary nature of the feedback constant, and vary only how that feedback is organised in space and time. A vertical layout ties the response closely to the key's own position, a radial layout turns it into a shape that grows from a shared centre, and a grid layout turns it into a pattern that moves independently of where the key sits.

This exploration is limited to Free Play, where visual feedback supports experimentation rather than performance assessment. Learning Mode is excluded because its visual feedback serves a different functional purpose: indicating the target note and whether the child’s input matches it.

Prototype 1: Dynamic Light Bars

Dynamic Light Bars draws on the visual language of audio waveforms and graphic equalisers. Each coloured key is connected to a fixed vertical bar, creating a direct spatial relationship between the keyboard and the display. A key press activates the corresponding bar, which rises and brightens while the note is held before settling when the key is released. Small controlled variations in height, segment pattern and movement speed prevent repeated presses from producing an identical image without changing the key's colour or position. This prototype explores a linear mapping in which simultaneous notes can be read as neighbouring columns, allowing the display to build a responsive visual rhythm from the child's timing and combinations of keys.

Prototype 2: Radial Pulse Network

This prototype moves the same reaction into a radial structure. Inspired by the radial structure of node-link diagrams, every visual event begins from the same central origin and uses the colour of the pressed key. The direction of each branch is randomly generated, allowing repeated presses of the same key to produce different spatial arrangements rather than returning to a fixed position. These choices connect the visual event to the timing and colour of the child’s action, while random direction introduces variation intended to support playful repetition and open-ended musical exploration.

Prototype 3: Colour Ripple Matrix

Inspired by the modular organisation of Sanya Matsokina's Generative Circle Matrix with the illuminated cell structure of a MIDI controller pad, this prototype spreads the reaction across a two-dimensional grid instead.

Each press lights a point at random within the grid and lets colour travel outward along its row and column, fading as it spreads and creating a ripple-like transmission. This prototype explores a distributed mapping in which a single note becomes a short spatial propagation across an ordered field.

AI Use Statement

Generative AI tools, including ChatGPT, Codex and AI image-generation tools, were used at different stages of this project. AI supported the definition and refinement of the two parallel prototype areas and six prototype directions, and provided suggestions for the website structure and its initial code framework. It generated and modified cartoon characters, poses, transparent-background assets and selected UI visuals in area1-prototype2, as well as rough visual mock-ups based on my descriptions for comparing and selecting design directions. Codex was also used to generate, revise and debug portions of the HTML, CSS and JavaScript, including the randomised visual feedback and animation logic. I reviewed, selected, modified and integrated these outputs and retained responsibility for the final design and implementation decisions.
